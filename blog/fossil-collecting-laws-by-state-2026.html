Is It Legal to Collect Fossils? Fossil Collecting Laws by State (2026)

By A&J Tech · Published October 8, 2026

This is not legal advice. Fossil collecting rules are a patchwork of federal, state, and local regulations that change, and some depend on the exact parcel of land you're standing on. Use this guide as a starting point, then verify with the land-management agency before you collect anything. When in doubt, leave it in the ground and report it.

The short version: on private land with the landowner's permission, fossil collecting is generally legal. On public land, it depends entirely on whose land it is, what kind of fossil it is, and which state you're in.

The federal baseline: the Paleontological Resources Preservation Act

The backbone of federal fossil law is the Paleontological Resources Preservation Act of 2009 (PRPA, 16 U.S.C. § 470aaa), part of the Omnibus Public Land Management Act. Here's how it works in practice:

  • Vertebrate fossils (dinosaurs, mammals, fish, reptiles) on federal land: collecting requires a permit, and permits go to qualified applicants for scientific or educational purposes — not casual hobbyists. Collected fossils remain U.S. property and are curated in approved public repositories. (NPS fossil protection page)
  • Casual collecting of common invertebrate and plant fossils (trilobites, leaf impressions, shells): allowed without a permit on lands administered by the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) in reasonable quantities for personal, noncommercial use. Current rules cap this at 25 pounds per person per day. It is prohibited on lands managed by the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. (DOI press release)
  • National Forest System lands: the Forest Service allows casual collection of common and abundant invertebrate and plant fossils for personal use without a permit, provided conditions in 36 CFR 291 are met. Commercial or vertebrate collecting requires a permit.
  • National parks: always no. NPS regulations (36 CFR § 2.1) prohibit possessing, removing, or disturbing paleontological specimens without a specimen-collection permit — and those permits are issued only to scientific or educational institutions for research purposes. Casual collecting is not allowed in any national park, period.
  • Petrified wood on BLM land: up to 25 pounds plus one piece per person per day, max 250 pounds per year. No selling without a permit. (BLM collecting guide)
  • Commercial fossil collecting on federal land: prohibited. Vertebrate fossils on federal land stay in public hands — they can't be bought or sold.

So if someone tells you "it's BLM land, everything's fair game" — they're half right at best. Trilobites, yes. That dinosaur bone, absolutely not.

State-by-state quick reference

What this table is: rules we could verify from primary sources (statutes, agency pages, official FAQs). For states we could not verify, the entry says Check state regulations — deliberately. We would rather understate than invent a law. Fossil rules sit with different agencies in different states (geological surveys, state land offices, parks departments), and they change.

State What we could verify
Alaska No collecting without a permit in national parks, national wildlife refuges, and state parks. Permits are for scientific research only. Private land with owner permission is allowed; on BLM land, check with the site office. (NPS Alaska)
Arizona On state-owned or state-controlled land, collecting vertebrate paleontological specimens requires a permit under ARS § 41-842; knowing excavation of fossil sites is prohibited without authorization. (ARS § 41-841)
California Prohibited in state parks — private collecting of rocks or fossils is barred (Public Resources Code §§ 4307, 5097.5). If you find a fossil in a state park, report it to park staff instead of moving it.
Florida Vertebrate fossils on state-owned or state-leased land require a fossil permit from the Florida Museum of Natural History. No collecting of any kind in state parks, national parks, wildlife refuges, or national forests. Collecting is generally allowed in navigable riverbeds (with some exemptions). Private land needs landowner permission — no permit required. (Florida Museum FAQ)
Kansas Commercial fossil hunters must get written landowner authorization — identifying themselves as commercial hunters — before entering land, and separate written authorization before removing any fossil (K.S.A. § 21-5811). Violations are misdemeanors.
Montana Fossils belong to the surface landowner, not the mineral-rights owner — settled by a 2019 state law and the 2020 Montana Supreme Court "Dueling Dinosaurs" ruling. If you're collecting in Montana with permission, you're generally dealing with the landowner. (Science)
Ohio Prohibited in state nature preserves and natural areas under the Department of Natural Resources — collecting fossils there requires a research permit (OAC 1501:17-3-02).
Utah Permit required on state-managed lands. Excavation or collection requires a permit from the Utah Geological Survey under Utah Code §§ 79-3-501/502; violations mean forfeiting everything you found plus penalties. (UGS permit form)
Wyoming On state trust lands, fossil collecting requires a permit from the Office of State Lands and Investments — either "Exclusive Commercial" or "Non-Exclusive Scientific." Scientific-permit specimens remain state property on loan. (OSLI)

States we could not verify from a current primary source (a 1989 Kansas Geological Survey survey of state rules suggests several have no specific fossil-collecting statute, but that's nearly 40 years old — verify locally before collecting): Alabama, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nebraska, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, West Virginia, Wisconsin.

The pattern to remember: in most states, collecting fossils is broadly prohibited in state parks and preserves even when there's no fossil-specific statute — general rules against removing natural objects cover it. Private land with permission is the safest ground in every state. Everything else, check first.

5 questions to ask before you collect

  1. Who owns this land? Private, state, federal — and if federal, which agency (BLM, Forest Service, NPS, Fish & Wildlife). This single question decides almost everything.
  2. Is it vertebrate or invertebrate? Vertebrate fossils trigger permit requirements almost everywhere public. If you can't tell, treat it as vertebrate.
  3. Do I have written permission? On private land, get the landowner's OK in writing. On public land, confirm with the managing agency's office — not a forum post.
  4. Is this a park, preserve, refuge, or monument? If yes, the answer is almost always no. Don't collect; photograph and report instead.
  5. Could this be archaeologically or culturally significant? Human-associated fossils and artifacts fall under different, stricter laws (ARPA, NAGPRA). If a fossil looks modified, associated with tools, or human — stop and report.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep a fossil I found?

It depends on where you found it and what it is. On private land with the landowner's permission, generally yes. On federal land, common invertebrate fossils collected casually from BLM or Forest Service land for personal use can usually be kept — but vertebrate fossils cannot be collected without a scientific permit and must go to a public repository. In national parks, nothing can be collected without an institutional research permit.

Is it illegal to collect fossils in national parks?

Yes. NPS regulations prohibit possessing, removing, or disturbing fossil specimens in national parks without a specimen-collection permit, and those permits are issued only to scientific or educational institutions for research. Casual collecting is never allowed in national parks.

Do I need a permit to collect fossils?

On federal land: yes for vertebrate fossils and commercial collecting; no for casual collection of common invertebrate and plant fossils on BLM and Forest Service lands (NPS and Fish & Wildlife lands excluded). Several states require permits on state land — Florida for vertebrate fossils on state land, Utah on state-managed land, Wyoming on state trust land, Arizona for vertebrate specimens on state land. Always check the specific state's rules.
